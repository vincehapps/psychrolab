The Rankine cycle — William John Macquorn Rankine's vapor power cycle, derived from the Carnot cycle — is the standard of comparison for steam power plants. It converts heat into work through four components and four processes, and once you can trace it on a T–s diagram, every improvement (reheat, regeneration, supercritical pressure) is a modification of this baseline.

## The four components

- **Boiler** — a heat exchanger; heat is added at constant pressure (isobaric) from coal, oil, natural gas or nuclear fission. Temperature and entropy both rise; water leaves as superheated steam.
- **Turbine** — work is generated by expanding steam; the process is isentropic (constant entropy) in the ideal case. Pressure and temperature fall; steam exits "spent", often as a wet mixture.
- **Condenser** — heat rejection must occur; another isobaric heat exchanger. Entropy decreases and the fluid reaches the saturated state — heat rejection at constant temperature is what makes the cycle efficient compared with exhaust-at-huge-temperature schemes.
- **Pump** — returns condensate to boiler pressure. Isentropic compression of a liquid; it **has the highest pressure in the cycle**. Work input here is small because pumping liquid costs far less than compressing vapor.

## The four processes on a T–s diagram

| Process | Component | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| 1 → 2 | Boiler | Isobaric heat addition, Q in, W = 0, s rises |
| 2 → 3 | Turbine | Isentropic expansion, s = const, W out |
| 3 → 4 | Condenser | Isobaric heat rejection, Q out, W = 0, phase change to saturated liquid |
| 4 → 1 | Pump | Isentropic compression, s = const, W in |

State 1 is compressed liquid entering the boiler; state 2 superheated vapor; state 3 a wet mixture (or saturated vapor); state 4 saturated liquid at condenser pressure.

## The performance metrics

```text
qin  = h2 − h1        heat added in the boiler  [kJ/kg]
wt   = h2 − h3        turbine work             [kJ/kg]
wp   = h1 − h4        pump work                [kJ/kg]
wnet = wt − wp
ηth  = wnet / qin
```

When the tables give no enthalpy at state 1, compute the pump term directly as `wp ≈ v4·(Pboiler − Pcondenser)`. Mass flow multiplies specific quantities into MW: `W = m·wnet`.

## Worked example

> A steam power plant operates a simple ideal Rankine cycle between 3.0 MPa and 50 kPa, turbine inlet 400 °C, steam flow 25 kg/s. Find thermal efficiency and net power.

State points:

- **State 2:** 3 MPa, 400 °C → from superheated tables, `h2`, `s2`.
- **State 3:** `s3 = s2` at 50 kPa. Quality `x3 = (s3 − sf)/sfg` at 50 kPa; `h3 = hf + x3·hfg`. This is the step that tells you how wet the exhaust is — a quality around 0.89 (as in the worked reheat example below) means roughly 11% of the flow is liquid droplets striking the last blades.
- **State 4:** saturated liquid at 50 kPa, `h4 = hf`.
- **State 1:** `h1 = h4 + v4·(P2 − P3)`.

Then `ηth = (w_t − w_p)/q_in` and `W = 25 kg/s × wnet`. Course problem sets for this cycle land in the 27–34% efficiency band depending on boiler pressure — 3.0 MPa/400 °C gives about 28.4% at 25 kg/s and ~20.5 MW output.

## Where the efficiency comes from

Rankine efficiency ultimately tracks the average temperature at which heat is **added** relative to the temperature at which it is **rejected**. The three levers:

1. **Lower the condenser pressure** — lowers the heat-rejection temperature. Limited by condenser vacuum and turbine exit moisture.
2. **Superheat the steam** — raises the average addition temperature without raising boiler pressure, and *decreases moisture at the turbine exit* — blade erosion is governed by steam quality.
3. **Raise boiler pressure** — raises the saturation temperature at which boiling occurs.

Each lever has a cost: higher pressure means thicker walls and more pump work; more superheat means hotter, costlier tubing; lower condenser pressure means bigger blades and wetter exhaust.
