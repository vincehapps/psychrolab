Indoor air quality (IAQ) is the quality of the air inside a building as represented by pollutant concentrations and the thermal conditions — temperature and relative humidity — that affect occupant health, comfort and performance. It is the half of air conditioning that temperature alone never delivers.

## What "acceptable" means

ASHRAE defines acceptable IAQ as air that:

- does not contain known contaminants at harmful concentrations as determined by cognizant authorities, and
- in which **80% or more of occupants** do not express dissatisfaction.

The 80% figure matters: it acknowledges that comfort perception varies, and it is the basis for the sensation-based methods used in ventilation standards such as ASHRAE 62.1.

## The contaminants

- Carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide
- Other gases and vapours
- Radioactive materials (notably radon)
- Microorganisms: bacteria, viruses, moulds, spores
- Allergens
- Suspended particulate matter: soot, smoke, silica, clay, decayed organic matter

Typical acceptable levels: **CO₂ ≤ 1000 ppm**, **CO ≤ 15 ppm**, **radon ≤ 4 pCi/L**, **VOCs ≤ 0.1 ppm**.

Symptoms of exposure cluster around headache, nausea and irritation of the eyes and nose — the signature of Sick Building Syndrome, where symptoms track time spent in the building and abate outside it.

## Where indoor pollutants come from

1. **VOCs** — benzene and trichloroethylene from paints, cleaners, room fresheners and aerosol sprays.
2. **Combustion products** — CO and NOₓ from cooking, smoking, firewood and heating equipment.
3. **Formaldehyde** — from carpets, plywood, building materials and adhesives.
4. **Biological contaminants** — mould and mildew thriving in damp areas, bedding and poorly maintained air conditioners.
5. **Asbestos** — from deteriorating ceilings, walls and pipe insulation.
6. **Respirable suspended particles** — house dust, pollen, animal hair.

## Control methods, in order of preference

1. **Source elimination or modification** — remove or seal the pollutant rather than dilute it.
2. **Outdoor air dilution** — ventilation carries contaminants below threshold. The trade-off is energy: ventilation load grows with every cubic metre of outdoor air, which is why demand-controlled ventilation and energy recovery matter.
3. **Space air distribution** — deliver clean air where people are; short-circuiting supply to exhaust leaves occupied zones stagnant.
4. **Air cleaning** — filters and sorbents for particles and gases.

### Gaseous contaminant removal mechanisms

- **Absorption** — contaminant dissolves into or reacts with the absorbing medium.
- **Adsorption** — molecules adhere to the surface of the medium (activated carbon is the standard example).
- **Chemisorption** — surface binding by chemical reaction.
- **Catalysis** — chemisorption on a catalyst surface.

## Why buildings fail

- Inadequate quantity of ventilation air.
- Load calculations based on assumed occupant, light and equipment densities that the real building exceeds.
- HVAC designed purely for thermal comfort at minimum energy, with outdoor air treated as a cost to be trimmed.
- IAQ ignored across design, installation, construction, operation and maintenance.

The economics cut one way: the cost of providing an optimum workplace environment is small compared with the productivity gain.

## Ventilation strategies

**Natural ventilation** — supply and removal through the building without mechanical systems, driven by pressure or temperature differences. Benefits: improved IAQ, energy savings, reduced greenhouse emissions, occupant control, fewer Sick Building Syndrome complaints, higher productivity.

**Mechanical ventilation** — fans and ducts deliver a controlled fresh-air flow with filtration, dehumidification and conditioning of the incoming air, independent of weather. The two are complementary: mixed-mode buildings use natural ventilation when conditions allow and mechanical systems when they do not.

## Practical building-side measures

- Ventilate regularly; use low-emission paints; never smoke indoors.
- Wet-mop to release less dust; avoid benzene-bearing cleaners (baking soda, vinegar and lime are safe substitutes).
- Reduce carpets (a formaldehyde and VOC source), vacuum them and clean vacuum filters regularly.
- Dry dishes and surfaces promptly — residual moisture feeds mould.
- Prefer pump sprays over aerosols.
- Avoid air fresheners masking odours; they emit VOCs themselves. Fragrance-free detergents, non-chlorine bleach, low-emission materials and non-toxic pest control round out the source-control list.

## The green building angle

Green building — environmentally responsible, resource-efficient practices across a building's life cycle — bundles IAQ with resource performance: reduced waste and pollution, efficient energy and water use, protected occupant health and productivity.

Air quality couples tightly to the psychrometric side: humidity control below ~70% RH retards mould, and filtration pressure drop shows up in fan power.
